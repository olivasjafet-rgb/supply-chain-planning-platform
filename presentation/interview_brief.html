The problem

Four questions, every month, for every item

Order management comes down to a small set of decisions that have to be made again and again, and are expensive in both directions when they are made badly:

  • How much will we sell? — by item, by month, far enough ahead to cover the lead time.
  • How much buffer does this item deserve? — too little loses the sale, too much locks up cash.
  • What do we order, from whom, and when? — respecting case packs, minimums and a 95-day ocean lane.
  • Who gets the units when there aren’t enough? — and which accounts must not be cut.

This platform answers all four in one run, and shows its work at every step. The numbers below are what it produces on the simulated book.

How it works

From two years of history to a signed purchase order

One command runs the whole cycle. Each stage hands the next one a finished answer, and every intermediate result is written out so a planner can audit any number back to its source.

Construction

How it is built

About 3,900 lines of Python — 2,000 of them the engine itself — structured so that the business rules live in one file and the logic lives in another. The engine is a set of small modules that each take dataframes in and hand dataframes out — no hidden state, so any stage can be run, inspected or replaced on its own.

config.yaml the only business-specific file │ ├─ src/config.py loads and validates the hierarchy definition ├─ src/dataio.py data in — CSV today, SQL or NetSuite tomorrow ├─ src/forecast.py seasonality, level, trend, accuracy scoring ├─ src/replenishment.py safety stock, min/max, time-phased plan ├─ src/allocation.py account mix, protected minimums, fair-share ├─ src/landed_cost.py supplier rates, savings gates, cost dilution ├─ src/kpis.py turns, GMROI, weeks of supply, ABC/XYZ └─ src/exports.py Excel workbook, Power BI schema, DAX run_simulation.py one full planning cycle run_scenarios.py seven scenarios, one input changed each app/app.py the interactive workbench tests/test_engine.py 21 tests

The product hierarchy is configuration, not code

Nothing in the engine hard-codes a product level, a location name, or a planning grain. The hierarchy is declared once and the code derives its grouping keys from it:

product_hierarchy: levels: - {key: division, label: "Division"} - {key: category, label: "Category"} - {key: subcategory, label: "Subcategory"} - {key: sku, label: "SKU"} planning_level: sku

A second profile in the repository proves this is real rather than aspirational: it runs a three-level hierarchy planning at category instead of SKU, with a different buffer method, a 9-month horizon and a binding cash cap — on the same engine, with no code changes. Re-pointing the platform at a different catalogue is a configuration exercise.

When the planning level sits above the data’s grain, attributes are aggregated the way each one behaves: lead time and cost demand-weighted, case pack takes the maximum, minimum order quantities sum. A plain average of lead times would quietly under-buffer every long-lead item in the group.

Built with AI, verified by measurement

This was written in partnership with Claude — not to generate code unattended, but to compress the loop that actually matters: write the logic, run it against data, read the result, challenge it. Working that way makes verification cheap enough to do at every step instead of at the end.

The evidence is that the model was caught being wrong three times before anyone else had to find out. Each one is now a regression test named after the failure it documents:

A seasonality rule that silently disabled itself 18% → 78% accuracy

A hard “needs two full years of history” gate saw 21 months ÷ 12 = 1.75 during the backtest and flattened every seasonal index to 1.0 — forecasting flat into a gardening business’s summer trough. Replacing the cliff with evidence-weighted shrinkage, where confidence grows gradually with the number of observations, fixed it.

Parameters that looked optimal against a single holdout +13% → −9% bias

Scored on one window the model read as over-forecasting; scored across six rolling origins, the same settings read as under-forecasting. The sign flipped. Only the rolling evaluation exposed the real cause underneath — a moving average describes the midpoint of its window, not today, which on a growing book reads as systematic under-forecast.

A cash cap applied after the plan instead of inside it 0 → 37 stockout months

Capping the monthly buy appeared to save $4.3M with no service impact whatsoever — because the inventory projection still contained the receipts the cap had cancelled. A constraint shared across competing items has to be applied while the plan is being built, ranking items by urgency against the budget, not filtered over a finished one.

None of those were visible from reading the code. They surfaced from running it and measuring it — which is the part of the workflow the AI accelerates most.

Trade-offs

What it costs to change your mind

A single plan answers “what should we buy?”. The question a supply chain lead actually gets asked is different, so the platform re-runs the identical engine with one input changed at a time. Any difference in the result is attributable to that input and nothing else.

Findings

Three things the model surfaced

Every projected stockout was unfixable by policy

All six shortages are long-lead imports falling in the first four months of the horizon. An order placed in month one lands in month four — so nothing inside the plan can reach them. They were locked in before the cycle started, by the opening inventory and whatever was already on the water.

That is why raising the service level leaves the shortage list untouched while adding inventory everywhere else. The platform now reports the split explicitly, because the two kinds carry opposite recommendations: locked shortages need expediting, substitution or demand shaping; addressable ones need a parameter change. Filed under one “stockout” heading, a planner spends the week fixing the wrong problem.

A sourcing saving reaches the P&L far later than assumed

Negotiating an origin surcharge out of supplier terms does not improve next month’s margin. The saving passes three gates, each with its own lag.

Under weighted-average costing, cheaper receipts dilute the pool they join rather than replacing it:

ratio(m) = [ Inv(m−1) × ratio(m−1) + Receipts_new(m) ] ÷ [ Inv(m−1) + Receipts_old(m) ]

So a four-month inventory turn does not mean the benefit is booked by month four — the ratio approaches its floor asymptotically. It is also not monotonic: a month weighted toward domestic supply admits receipts carrying no surcharge, above an already-discounted pool, and the reported saving moves backwards. Worth knowing before someone reads a single month as a regression.

Supplier cost rates have to be measured, not assumed

Each supplier’s origin surcharge is computed from its own actual expenses divided by its own actual purchase value. Across this book the real spread runs from 0% to 6.7%, weighting to 4.68% overall. A single blended assumption would misprice every supplier in the list — overstating the domestic vendors, who carry none of it, and understating the long-lane imports, who carry the most.

The stack

Streamlit for deciding, Power BI for distributing

The front end is a Streamlit application, and it gets mistaken for Power BI often enough that the distinction is worth stating plainly. A BI tool filters numbers that already exist somewhere. Nothing in the order book exists until the engine solves for it — there is no table anywhere containing “order 14,400 units of BTR-1001 in November”. That row is the output of a projection.

Both belong in the stack, and this project ships both. Every run also writes a clean star schema and a measures.dax file, because publishing to a wide audience is genuinely Power BI’s job. Python does the solving; Power BI does the distribution.

In the workbench, every sidebar control re-runs the full engine and redraws from the new result. Ticking Cap monthly purchases recomputes 26 SKUs across 12 months and moves the fill rate from 99.7% to 92.5% in about a second. No BI filter can do that, because the answer is not in any table until it has been computed.

In production

The same problem, already running on Power BI

The engine above is a simulation. Alongside it I build and maintain a production Power BI report for a national retail and distribution business — a daily min/max evaluation that answers one question per SKU per location:

We are below the minimum. Where does the cover come from — transfer it from the distribution centre, wait for what is already in transit, or raise a purchase order?

Separating transfer from buy is the whole point. Both look identical in a raw “below minimum” report, and treating them the same means buying inventory the company already owns — paying twice for the same cover while the original units age at the DC.

The measure that matters most caps coverage per item before summing. The naive version — total available ÷ total required — lets surplus on one SKU silently offset a shortage on another, so a location with triple cover on one item and nothing on a second reads as fully covered. Capping at MIN(available, required) inside a SUMX over each SKU makes the number mean what a planner thinks it means. The same pattern, run against three different supply sets, produces the sentence the morning meeting actually needs: “71% today, 88% once the transit lands, 96% if we also pull from the DC.”

Documented separately as a case study — architecture, the four DAX techniques it rests on, and a model-health audit I ran against it, including what I would change next. It carries no figures, identities, hosts or paths from the business it serves.

Method

Choices I would defend in a review

  • Seasonality is estimated one level up. A single SKU has two observations per calendar month in a 24-month history — far too few for a stable index. Its category has twenty or more. Accept a little bias to remove a lot of variance.
  • Weak estimates are shrunk, not discarded. Confidence in a seasonal index grows with the number of times that month has actually been observed. Nothing is thrown away; thin evidence is pulled toward neutral in proportion to how thin it is.
  • Safety stock carries both variances — demand and lead time. Dropping the lead-time term is the most common way to under-stock a long-lead import: on a 95-day lane swinging ±15 days, that term dominates.
  • Item health is judged against the item’s own supply chain. A 95-day import holding 18 weeks of cover is running correctly; a 14-day domestic SKU holding 18 weeks is not. One company-wide threshold is wrong about half the catalogue.
  • Minimum presentation stock is protected before fair-share. Straight pro-rata quietly drops retail doors below the level where the shelf is lost, and losing the shelf costs far more than the units.
  • Planner overrides never destroy the statistical value. Both are stored, so forecast error can be attributed to the model or to the human, and any override can be rolled back.
  • Accuracy is reported with its spread. 78.0% ±3.0% across six folds, worst fold 74.4%. A model that swings between 60% and 90% needs different governance than one that sits at 78% every month, and a single number hides that.

The data is simulated with a fixed seed, so every figure here regenerates from python run_simulation.py. Supplier names are anonymised; demand shapes, lead times, case packs and cost rates are set to realistic ranges for the category. No proprietary data from any employer appears anywhere in the project.
